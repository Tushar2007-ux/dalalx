01

Start here

Uncross is a pretend stock exchange running on a computer. People and robots send in "I want to buy" and "I want to sell" requests, and the program pairs them up into trades, fairly and very fast. The website lets you watch all of it happen, styled like a financial newspaper.

That is the whole idea. Everything else in this guide is detail on top of that one sentence. Read it in order the first time, because each part builds on the one before.

Your one-line opening"An exchange is a matchmaker for buyers and sellers. We built one from scratch in Java, and this website lets you watch it think."

Why it is called Uncross

When a buyer is willing to pay at least what a seller is asking, their prices "cross". The engine's job is to settle that crossing by turning it into a trade. "Uncrossing" the book is the real exchange term for exactly this job, so the name describes what the program does.

Why it looks like a newspaper

The theme is called "The Morning Edition". Each page of the site is a section of a financial paper: a Front Page with headlines, a Trading Floor, Correspondents (the bots), Investigations (catching cheaters). There is a light Day edition and a dark Night edition. The design is only clothing; the important part is the engine underneath.

02

The stock market in plain words

What is a share?

A company like Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) is split into many small pieces called shares. Owning one share means owning a tiny piece of the company. People buy shares hoping the price goes up, and sell them when they want the money back.

What is a stock exchange?

A place (today, a computer) where buyers and sellers of shares meet. In India the real ones are the NSE and BSE. The exchange does not buy or sell anything itself; it only matches buyers with sellers and records the trades. Think of it as a marketplace manager standing between two crowds.

What is an order?

An order is a message saying what you want. For example: "Buy 100 shares of RELIANCE, and I will pay at most ₹102.50 each." It has four parts: which stock, buy or sell, how many (quantity), and usually what price.

What is the order book?

Most orders cannot trade the moment they arrive, because nobody on the other side wants that price yet. Those orders wait in a list called the order book. Every stock has its own book with two sides:

Bids (buyers)

People who want to buy, and the highest price each will pay. The best bid is the highest one. Shown in green.

Asks (sellers)

People who want to sell, and the lowest price each will accept. The best ask is the lowest one. Shown in red.

The gap between the best ask and the best bid is the spread. While the best buyer wants to pay less than the best seller wants to receive, nothing trades. Everyone waits.

ORDER BOOK · RELIANCE
ASK102.60300a seller wants ₹102.60
ASK102.50200best ask (cheapest seller)
— the spread: 10 paise —
BID102.40400best bid (highest buyer)
BID102.30250a buyer offering ₹102.30

How a trade happens: the matching rule

Now someone sends "Buy 500 at ₹102.50." Step through what the engine does. Use the arrows, or press Play.

Notice: the trade happens at ₹102.50, the price of the order that was already waiting.

The one rule everything follows: price, then time

Better price goes first. A seller asking less is served before a seller asking more. At the same price, whoever came first goes first, like a queue at a ticket counter. This is called price–time priority, and it is how real exchanges are fair. Our engine follows it exactly.

Money is counted in paise, never with decimals

In the code, ₹102.50 is stored as the whole number 10250 (paise). Computers cannot store numbers like 0.1 exactly when they use decimals (double), so tiny errors creep in. On an exchange doing thousands of trades, those tiny errors add up to real money. Whole numbers of paise are always exact. If anyone asks "how do you store money?", this is the answer, and it is a good one.

03

The five kinds of order

Each kind of order is a different instruction about how patient you are and how much you want to show.

OrderIn plain wordsEveryday comparison
Limit"Buy at this price or better. If nobody will sell that cheap yet, I will wait in the book."Telling a shopkeeper "call me if it drops to ₹500".
Market"Buy right now at whatever the best price is." Trades immediately, never waits.Paying the price on the tag, no bargaining.
IOC (Immediate or Cancel)"Trade whatever you can right now at my price; cancel the rest. Do not leave me waiting.""Give me as many as you have at this price, and I'm leaving."
Stop-loss"If the price falls to ₹1,690, sell my shares." It hides until the price reaches the trigger, then acts like a market order.A safety alarm that sells before you lose too much.
IcebergA big order that shows only a small slice at a time. When the slice is used up, the next slice appears.Only the tip of an iceberg is visible above water.

Two fairness details worth knowing

  • Iceberg slices go to the back of the queue. Otherwise a big fund could show 100 shares, stand first in line, and quietly fill 900 more ahead of everyone. Each new slice re-joins the line at the back.
  • Stops can chain. One stop selling pushes the price down, which can trigger the next stop, and the next. That is how crashes feed themselves. The engine follows the chain, but stops it the moment the circuit breaker halts the stock.

Amending an order

You can change an order that is waiting. If you only reduce the quantity at the same price, you keep your place in the queue. If you change the price or ask for more shares, it counts as cancelling and re-entering, so you go to the back. Otherwise you could hold your place in line while changing what you are in line for.

A strong point to make"When we added stop-loss and iceberg, we did not edit a single existing matcher. We wrote two new classes and added two lines to a registry. That is the Open/Closed principle working in real code."
04

What Uncross is made of

Three pieces of software, each with one job, plus the people and robots who trade on it.

The engine (core)

Pure Java 21. Holds the order books, does the matching, runs the bots, the circuit breaker, the cheat detection. It knows nothing about websites. This is the brain.

The server

Spring Boot. Only a translator: it takes web requests, hands them to the engine, and streams the engine's news back to browsers over a WebSocket (a live connection). No business logic lives here.

The website (frontend)

React and TypeScript. Draws the newspaper. It only shows what the engine already decided; it never decides anything itself.

The five stocks

RELIANCE, TCS, INFY, HDFCBANK, TATAMOTORS. These are real company names, but the prices are simulated, not real market data. You can also list new stocks while it runs (up to 12 in total).

06

The special systems

Circuit breaker: the emergency brake

If a stock moves 10% within 60 seconds, up or down, trading in it halts for 30 seconds. Real exchanges (NSE, NYSE) do this too.

Why: not to stop people losing money, but to break a panic loop. Falling prices scare people into selling, which makes prices fall further. A pause lets everyone breathe and think.

OPEN→ 10% move in 60 s →HALTED→ 30 s →AUCTION→ 30 s →OPEN

Each stage is its own object (the State pattern). The code never asks "are we halted?" with if-statements everywhere; it asks the current state what is allowed.

Call auction: reopening fairly

While halted, orders are collected but none trade. At the reopen, instead of letting the fastest trader grab the best deal, the engine finds the one price at which the most shares can trade, and everyone trades at that single price.

Simple picture: buyers form a demand curve (more buyers at lower prices), sellers form a supply curve (more sellers at higher prices). Where the two curves cross is the auction price. The Trading Floor draws these curves live during an auction.

Why it is fair"If trading just restarted, whoever sends their order first wins the price gap. With one auction price for everybody, arriving first stops mattering."

Surveillance: five ways to cheat, five rules

RuleThe cheat, in plain words
SpoofingPlacing huge orders you never intend to fill, to fool others about demand, then cancelling them all at once.
LayeringStacking many fake orders on several prices next to each other to make the book look deeper than it is.
Wash tradesTrading with yourself (you are both buyer and seller) to fake activity.
PumpOne account pushing a price up through its own buying.
Front-runningBuying just before someone else's big order (because you knew it was coming) and selling into it at a profit.

The journal: nothing is lost on a restart

Every command that changes a book is written to a file, one line each, like a diary. If the server stops and starts again, it reads the diary from the top and plays every command through the same engine, rebuilding the exact same market. Measured: 45,039 commands replayed in 2.1 seconds.

Two tricks make the replay exact: the clock is pinned to each entry's original time, so trades keep their timestamps; and because order numbers come out differently the second time, the replayer keeps a table mapping old numbers to new ones. Rewind uses the same diary.

The honest limit: the diary is written by a background thread, so a sudden crash can lose the last few milliseconds. A real exchange waits for the disk before confirming. Say this yourself before someone asks; it shows you understand the trade-off.

The risk desk

Before an order reaches the book, the risk desk checks the account: enough free cash for a buy, enough shares for a sell. If yes, it sets the money or shares aside. After a trade it settles the money; on a cancel it gives back what was set aside. The check and the settlement run on the stock's own worker, one after the other, so they can never get out of step.

Simulated news

Every 60 to 150 seconds, a made-up company story breaks ("TCS wins a large contract"). It changes what the bots think a stock is worth, and the bots react, so the price moves for a reason, just like real markets reacting to news. A chime plays when news breaks.

Headlines and sound

A headline writer turns engine events into newspaper sentences for the Front Page. Sounds add atmosphere: a typewriter clack for each trade on the tape, a bell, a stamp for a ruling, a chime for news.

08

Under the hood, explained simply

The bank counter analogy: one clerk per stock

Imagine a bank with five counters, one per stock. Each counter has exactly one clerk, and only that clerk may touch that counter's ledger (the order book). Customers do not walk behind the counter; they drop their request slip into the counter's tray, and the clerk handles slips one at a time, in order.

The clerk

= one worker thread per stock (SymbolWorker).

The tray

= a BlockingQueue: many people can put slips in safely at once.

The ledger

= the order book, deliberately not thread-safe, because only its own clerk ever touches it.

This is called thread confinement. Because only one thread touches a book, there is no need for locks inside it, so there are no deadlocks and no half-finished updates. Five stocks run in parallel on five threads, but each book is always handled by one.

  • Producer–consumer: many producers (web requests, bots, phones) put commands in the tray; one consumer (the clerk) takes them out.
  • Back-pressure: if a tray is full, a new request waits up to one second for space instead of piling up forever. The system slows down gracefully instead of falling over.
  • Getting an answer back: when you send an order you get a CompletableFuture, a receipt that fills in with the result once the clerk has processed your slip.

Events: the engine announces, everyone listens

Every time something happens (an order accepted, a trade, a cancel, a halt), the engine publishes an event. The chart, the tape, X-Ray, surveillance, the headline writer, the journal and the risk desk all listen to the same stream. The engine does not know or care who is listening. This is the Observer pattern, and it is why so many features could be added without touching the engine: each one is just another listener.

The journey of one order

Phone / browser→Server (REST)→Risk check→Stock's queue→Worker matches→Events→WebSocket→Every screen

Why three separate modules?

The engine (core) has no Spring import anywhere. Dependencies point inward only: the server uses the engine, never the other way round. So the engine can be tested on its own in seconds (415 Java tests), and you could swap the website for a desktop app without changing one line of the engine. That is the Dependency Inversion idea.

The OOP design patterns

This is an Object-Oriented Modelling course, so this part matters most for marks. For each pattern: the idea in one line, and where it lives in Uncross.

PatternThe ideaWhere in Uncross
StrategySwap the "how" without changing the code that uses it.Each order type has its own matcher class; each bot has its own brain; order checks (gates) are interchangeable.
FactoryOne place that knows how to build objects correctly.Order creation goes through a factory, so every order is valid and gets an id and timestamp.

10

The speed story

The strongest moment in the presentation. Tell it as a short story in three beats.

  1. We measured. The first benchmark managed only 3,485 orders per second. Embarrassingly slow.
  2. The cause was a surprise. Matching was fine. The slow part was X-Ray: to name the biggest trader at each price, it walked through every single order waiting at that price, tens of thousands of steps per order.
  3. One fix. Keep a running total of shares per trader at each price instead of recounting. After that: 169,315 orders per second, about 48× faster, with every test still passing.

End to end through the queues: 72,380 orders per second, a typical order takes 43 microseconds.

The lesson to say out loud"Measure before you optimise. Everything we would have guessed was already fast. The slow part was the feature that explains the engine, not the engine."

If asked "is 169,000 good?": for one thread on a small machine in Java, it is reasonable. Real exchanges do millions with special hardware. What matters here is the 48× improvement and how we found it.

Running the live demo

Starting it

  1. Check Java: run java -version; it must say 21 or higher. Node 20 or higher is also needed.
  2. In the project folder, run ./run.sh. It starts the server on port 8080 and the website on port 5173. The first run takes a few minutes while things download; after that it takes seconds.
  3. Open http://localhost:5173 in the browser.
  4. Press Ctrl+C in the terminal to stop both.

13

Questions you may be asked

Click a question to see a good answer. Practise saying them in your own words.

Why not use double for prices?

A double cannot store most decimal numbers exactly (0.1 is stored as 0.1000000000000000055…). Over thousands of trades the errors add up. We store paise as a long, so ₹102.50 is exactly 10250, and all arithmetic is exact.

Why one thread per stock instead of locks?

Locks are easy to get wrong: deadlocks, forgotten locks, slow contention. If only one thread ever touches a book, the book needs no locks at all. Different stocks still run in parallel on their own threads. Matching on one stock never needs data from another, so splitting by stock costs nothing.

What happens if many orders arrive at the same instant?

They all go into the stock's queue, which is thread-safe, and the worker processes them one at a time in arrival order. That gives a clear, fair order to "same time" requests. If the queue fills up, senders wait up to one second (back-pressure) instead of overloading memory.

Your order book is not thread-safe. Isn't that a bug?

It is a deliberate design choice called thread confinement. The book is only ever accessed by its own worker thread, so making it thread-safe would add cost and give nothing. The comments in the code explain this wherever it matters.

How would you add a new order type?

Write one new matcher class that implements the matching Strategy interface and register it with one line. That is exactly how stop-loss and iceberg were added; no existing matcher was edited. Open/Closed principle.

Where is the Observer pattern?

The engine publishes events (order accepted, trade, cancel, halt). Listeners such as the chart, tape, X-Ray, surveillance, headline writer, journal and risk desk subscribe. The engine does not know who is listening, so new features are added as new listeners.

Where is the State pattern?

Each stock's market status is a state object: Open, Halted, or Auction. Each state decides what is allowed (for example, Halted accepts no trades; Auction accepts only limit orders). The circuit breaker switches the state object instead of setting flags.

How does the call auction pick its price?

It looks at every candidate price and counts how many shares could trade there: buyers willing to pay at least that much against sellers willing to accept at most that much. It picks the price where that number is largest, which is where the demand and supply curves cross. Everyone who trades gets that one price.

Can surveillance accuse someone wrongly?

Yes, and we say so. Once, the front-running rule flagged the Momentum bot, whose behaviour genuinely fit the pattern. That is why a rule only opens a case with its evidence; a person makes the ruling. Real exchanges work the same way.

What if the server crashes?

On start-up it replays the journal through the same engine and rebuilds every book exactly, before the website opens or bots start. 45,039 commands took 2.1 seconds. The limit: the journal is written by a background thread, so the last few milliseconds before a crash can be lost. A real exchange waits for the disk before confirming each order; that would be the next improvement.

How do you stop someone spending the same money twice?

When a buy order is accepted, its full cost is set aside immediately, reducing "free cash". A second order can only use what is still free. Because the check and the later settlement run on the same worker thread one after another, they can never interleave.

Why is the server separate from core?

So the engine depends on nothing about the web. It can be tested alone in seconds, and the web layer could be replaced without changing the engine. The server only translates HTTP and WebSocket messages into engine calls; it holds no business logic.

Why records?

Records are immutable: once created they cannot change. Events and trades are shared between threads (the worker publishes, listeners read), and immutable objects are always safe to share. They also give equals, hashCode and toString for free.

How fast is it, and how did you know?

We wrote a benchmark with fixed random seeds and a warm-up round, so results repeat. The engine alone does about 169,000 orders per second, a median of 4.6 microseconds per order. The first run was 48 times slower; profiling showed the X-Ray feature was the bottleneck, and one data-structure change fixed it.

How did you test it?

415 Java tests with JUnit 5 and AssertJ, and 239 frontend tests. Each feature was built tests-first. Matching rules, partial fills, the breaker, the auction, surveillance rules, recovery and the risk desk all have their own tests.

What would you add next?

Waiting for the disk before confirming orders (true durability), real authentication instead of a PIN, more order types such as good-till-date, and running across several machines.
